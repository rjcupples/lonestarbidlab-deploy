North Texas — Underground Utilities

I color on plans and play with other people's money all day, and build tools when the software pisses me off.

I'm Ryan Cupples. I bid underground utility work in North Texas—water, sewer, storm, excavation, paving. Nights and weekends I build software to make the work less annoying.

Lone Star Bid Lab isn't a company. It's not a side business. It's just what I call the space where I work on stuff—the bidding, the tools, the experiments.

The Work

What My Days Look Like

Most of my time goes to estimating and bidding underground utility projects. That means reading civil plans, doing takeoffs, chasing material quotes, pricing labor and equipment, and putting together bids that win work without leaving money on the table.

The actual construction—waterlines, sanitary sewer, storm drainage, excavation, paving restoration—that's what the numbers are about. I've done enough of it to know what things actually cost and what goes wrong.

A Typical Day

  • Reviewing plans and specs for new bid invitations
  • Quantity takeoffs—pipe lengths, fittings, manholes, trench volumes
  • Calling suppliers for material pricing
  • Building labor and equipment estimates
  • Figuring out what the spec requires vs. what's standard
  • Deciding which jobs are worth chasing
  • Submitting bids and waiting to see if the number hit

The Lab

Tools I Tinker With

Estimating software is expensive, clunky, or both. So I build my own stuff when something's broken enough to be worth fixing. These aren't products—just things I've duct-taped together because the alternative was doing it manually.

AI agents for bid day

Scripts that read bid tabs, pull spec requirements, organize addenda, flag the stuff I'd miss at 2am.

Takeoff helpers

Spreadsheets and scripts to speed up quantity work. Less clicking, more thinking about whether the numbers make sense.

Random automation

If I do something tedious more than twice, I'll probably try to automate it. Sometimes it works.
